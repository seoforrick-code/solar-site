How the calculation works

A simplified planning estimate can be calculated using:

Required panel watts = Daily Wh ÷ Peak sun hours ÷ Estimated system efficiency

The efficiency factor is intended to represent normal losses from things such as heat, wiring, conversion, dust, panel orientation, battery charging, and other system-level factors.

This calculator uses 80% as a planning assumption. It is not a guaranteed real-world efficiency for every installation.

Actual performance can be higher or lower depending on the equipment, installation, weather, temperature, shading, wiring, battery charging conditions, and other factors.

Example calculation

Suppose you use 1,500 Wh per day and receive 4.5 peak sun hours:

1,500 Wh ÷ 4.5 hours ÷ 0.80 ≈ 417 W

Because solar panels are normally purchased in fixed sizes, the installed array should generally meet or exceed the calculated planning requirement. With 400 W panels, for example, 2 panels would provide 800 W of rated capacity.

That does not mean the array will continuously produce 800 W. Panel ratings are measured under standardized test conditions, while real-world output changes throughout the day.

Peak sun hours vs. daylight hours

Peak sun hours are not simply the number of hours that the sun is above the horizon.

Solar resource is commonly expressed as equivalent hours of sunlight at a reference irradiance of 1,000 W/m². A location could have many hours of daylight while receiving considerably fewer equivalent peak-sun hours.

This distinction matters when sizing an off-grid solar system. Using total daylight hours instead of peak sun hours can substantially underestimate the panel capacity required.

Seasonal variation matters

Solar production can change significantly throughout the year. A location that averages a certain number of peak sun hours annually may receive substantially less solar energy during its cloudiest or shortest winter months.

If your system needs to operate reliably year-round, consider using a conservative solar resource estimate for the season that matters most rather than relying only on an annual average.

Important: A system designed around an annual average may produce more energy than necessary during favorable months but still struggle to fully recharge a battery during extended periods of poor solar conditions.

Clouds, shade and real-world output

Solar panels rarely operate at their nameplate rating for an entire day. Output is affected by cloud cover, shading, panel temperature, orientation, soiling, wiring, and the electrical characteristics of the rest of the system.

Even a correctly sized array can produce much less energy during heavily overcast weather. For an off-grid system, battery capacity can provide a buffer for periods when solar production is temporarily low.

Panel types and efficiency

Panel technology can affect how much physical space is required for a given array size, but the most important number for this calculator is the panel's rated power under its stated test conditions.

  • Monocrystalline: commonly used where high power density and limited installation space matter.
  • Polycrystalline: an older panel technology that can still be suitable for some installations where space and price are less restrictive.
  • Flexible and portable panels: useful when portability or unusual mounting requirements matter, but their construction and operating conditions can differ from rigid permanent panels.

When comparing panels, check the manufacturer's rated power, Voc, Vmp, Isc, Imp, dimensions, temperature characteristics, warranty, and installation requirements rather than relying only on marketing claims.

Can you mix different solar panels?

Panels with different electrical characteristics can sometimes be used in the same solar system, but the way they are connected matters.

Panels connected in series operate with the same current through the string, while their voltages add. Panels connected in parallel operate at approximately the same voltage, while their currents add.

Because of this, mixing panels with significantly different electrical characteristics can affect system performance and configuration.

If you are combining different panel models, check their Voc, Vmp, Isc and Imp and make sure the resulting configuration stays within the voltage, current, and power limits of the MPPT controller or inverter.

Common questions

How many solar panels do I need for 1,000 Wh per day?

It depends on your peak sun hours and system losses. For example, using 4.5 peak sun hours and an 80% planning efficiency factor gives approximately 278 W of required array capacity. In practice, you would round up to an appropriate panel configuration, such as one 400 W panel or multiple smaller panels depending on the system design.

How many solar panels do I need for 2,000 Wh per day?

At 4.5 peak sun hours and an 80% planning efficiency factor, the calculation gives approximately 556 W. The actual panel configuration depends on the available panel sizes and the electrical limits of the controller or inverter.

Do I need more panel wattage than my battery's rated capacity?

Not necessarily. Battery capacity and solar array capacity describe different parts of the system. The battery determines how much stored energy is available, while the solar array determines how much energy can be generated during charging periods.

What happens if I use too few solar panels?

The system may still operate, but it may take longer to recharge the battery. During periods of poor weather or low winter solar production, a small array may not generate enough energy to replace your daily consumption.

What angle should solar panels be installed at?

A fixed panel's optimal tilt depends on latitude, season, orientation, shading, and the goals of the system. A simple latitude-based rule can provide a starting point, but location-specific solar modeling is better for a permanent installation.

Can this calculator guarantee my solar system will produce enough energy?

No. This is a planning calculator, not a professional engineering design tool. Actual production depends on weather, shading, temperature, panel orientation, equipment efficiency, wiring, battery charging conditions, and other factors.

Methodology and limitations

This calculator uses a simplified energy-balance approach:

Daily energy requirement ÷ peak sun hours ÷ assumed system efficiency = estimated array wattage.

The default 80% factor is a planning assumption intended to account for typical system-level losses. It should not be interpreted as a guaranteed efficiency value for every solar installation.

A complete system design should also verify the solar controller or inverter's maximum PV voltage, current, power, battery charging limits, and other manufacturer specifications.
