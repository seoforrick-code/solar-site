LiFePO4 vs. AGM batteries: which is right for off-grid solar?

Every watt-hour figure on this site assumes a battery — but "1,000 Wh" means very different things depending on the chemistry inside it. Here's what generally separates lithium from lead-acid, and where each one still makes sense.

Quick comparison reference

Typical ranges seen across common product lines — actual specifications vary by manufacturer and model. Always check your specific battery's datasheet.

Cycle life and safe depth of discharge vary meaningfully by manufacturer, cell quality, battery management system (BMS), temperature, and how the battery is used. Always check your specific battery's datasheet for its rated figures.

Why lithium has become common in modern solar generators

While AGM lead-acid batteries have been used for decades in off-grid setups, Lithium Iron Phosphate (LiFePO4) has become standard in most modern portable power stations and a large share of DIY builds. A major reason is depth of discharge: many LiFePO4 batteries are designed for routine use in the 80–100% usable range, while lead-acid is more commonly limited to around 50% to avoid shortening its lifespan. The exact usable range for any given battery depends on its manufacturer and BMS settings, so this is a general tendency rather than a fixed rule.

Put concretely: a "1,000 Wh" AGM battery commonly delivers somewhere around 500 Wh of practical use before deeper discharge starts meaningfully shortening its lifespan. A "1,000 Wh" LiFePO4 battery often delivers closer to 800–1,000 Wh of real usable energy, depending on the specific product. If you're comparing two products by their rated Wh alone without accounting for this, you're not necessarily comparing them on equal terms.

Cycle life is where the cost math can flip

AGM batteries are typically cheaper to buy but tend to wear out faster — often rated for a few hundred full discharge cycles before capacity noticeably degrades, though this varies with depth of discharge, temperature, and charge rate. LiFePO4 batteries are commonly rated for several thousand cycles, usually measured to 80% of original capacity remaining rather than total failure. If you calculate cost per cycle rather than just upfront price, lithium is frequently the more economical option over a multi-year horizon for systems cycled daily, even though the initial purchase price is typically higher. Always compare the manufacturer's specific cycle rating and test conditions rather than assuming a generic figure applies to your product.

Weight matters more than people expect

For portable use — camping, vanlife, an emergency kit you need to carry — the weight difference is often significant, since LiFePO4 cells generally have a higher energy density than lead-acid. For a stationary installation this matters less, but for anything you need to lift, mount, or transport regularly, it's often a deciding factor on its own. Check the actual weight listed on the specific product you're considering, since this varies by capacity and manufacturer.

Where AGM can still make sense

Lithium isn't automatically the right choice for every situation. AGM lead-acid can remain a reasonable option when:

  • Budget is the primary constraint and the system will see light, occasional use rather than daily deep cycling.
  • Cold-weather charging is a concern and you can't add a battery with built-in low-temperature protection — many standard LiFePO4 cells shouldn't be charged below freezing without it, while AGM generally tolerates cold charging conditions better, though at reduced capacity.
  • Replacing an existing lead-acid system where the charge controller and wiring are already built around AGM's voltage characteristics.
  • Weight isn't a constraint — a stationary installation where the battery never needs to move again after setup.

What this means for sizing your system

If you've used the generator sizing calculator on this site, note that its battery tier recommendations generally assume lithium chemistry with a fairly deep usable discharge range. If you're building a lead-acid based system instead, it's common to roughly double the rated battery capacity to get a comparable amount of real-world usable energy — but confirm this against your specific battery's own DoD rating rather than relying on a fixed multiplier.

Common questions

Can I mix LiFePO4 and AGM batteries in the same bank?

This generally isn't recommended. The two chemistries have different charge voltage profiles and discharge curves, so a charge controller optimized for one will typically undercharge or overcharge the other, and the batteries can discharge unevenly within the bank. Keeping chemistries as separate battery banks is the safer default if you need to use both.

How long do LiFePO4 batteries actually last in real use?

Manufacturer cycle ratings are usually measured to 80% of original capacity remaining, not total failure — meaning many LiFePO4 batteries continue functioning, just at reduced capacity, beyond their rated cycle count. Real-world lifespan depends heavily on the specific product, how it's used, and operating temperature, so treat manufacturer figures as a reference point rather than a guarantee.

Do I need a special charge controller for lithium?

Generally yes — the charge controller needs to be configured (or purpose-built) for LiFePO4's voltage profile, which differs from lead-acid. Most modern MPPT controllers include a lithium charging profile as a selectable option; older lead-acid-only controllers may not, and using an incorrectly configured controller can undercharge the battery or trip its protection circuitry.

Is LiFePO4 safe compared to other lithium battery types?

LiFePO4 is generally considered a comparatively stable lithium chemistry, with a lower tendency toward thermal runaway than some other lithium chemistries used in laptops or EVs. That said, safety in any specific product also depends on cell quality, the battery management system, the charger used, installation, temperature, and wiring — chemistry alone doesn't guarantee safety.
