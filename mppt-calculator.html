CHARGE CONTROLLER & FUSE SIZING

What size MPPT controller does your array need — and what a PV fuse actually requires

Controller sizing can be estimated reliably from watts and voltage alone. PV fuse sizing can't — it depends on your specific panel's short-circuit current and how your strings are wired. This page gives you a solid controller estimate and walks you through what a fuse calculation actually needs.

How controller sizing is calculated

Estimated operating current = Array watts ÷ System voltage. A 25% safety margin (in line with typical continuous-load guidance) is added on top, then rounded up to the nearest common controller size (30A, 40A, 60A, 80A, 100A) as a starting reference point.

This estimate tells you roughly what current your array will produce at your battery voltage — but it does not replace checking the actual controller model you're considering. Every MPPT controller has its own maximum PV input voltage and maximum PV input current limits, and your array's open-circuit voltage (Voc) and short-circuit current (Isc) — not just its wattage — need to stay within those limits, especially in cold weather when panel Voc rises above its rated value. Always cross-check your specific panel's datasheet against your specific controller's datasheet before wiring anything.

Why voltage matters as much as wattage

The same array wattage produces very different current depending on your system voltage. An 800 W array on a 12 V system draws roughly 67 A, but the same 800 W array on a 24 V system draws only about 33 A — half the current, which generally means thinner wire and a smaller controller. This is a common reason larger off-grid systems move to 24 V or 48 V as they scale — 12 V wiring becomes impractically thick once you're pushing more than a kilowatt or so of panels.

PV fuse sizing: what it actually needs

Unlike controller sizing, a correct PV fuse or breaker rating cannot be reliably calculated from array wattage and system voltage alone — that's a common oversimplification, and using it as your final number can leave wiring under-protected or a fuse that nuisance-trips. Proper PV fuse sizing depends on several things specific to your actual hardware:

  • Module short-circuit current (Isc) — found on your specific panel's datasheet, not estimated from wattage.
  • Number of parallel strings — each additional parallel string adds its own Isc to the total fault current the fuse needs to handle.
  • The module's maximum series fuse rating — most panel datasheets specify this directly; it exists specifically to protect the panel itself from reverse current in a fault condition.
  • Conductor ampacity — the wire gauge between panels and controller has its own safe current limit that the fuse must protect, not just the panels.
  • Applicable electrical code — in the US, NEC Article 690 governs PV system overcurrent protection specifics.

PWM vs. MPPT — why the controller type matters

There are two families of solar charge controller, and they aren't interchangeable in performance:

  • PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) controllers are cheaper but essentially pull panel voltage down to match battery voltage, wasting the difference as heat. They can work reasonably well in small, closely voltage-matched systems but generally lose efficiency otherwise.
  • MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) controllers actively convert excess panel voltage into extra current, which can meaningfully improve harvested energy compared to PWM, particularly when panel voltage is well above battery voltage.

The exact efficiency gap between PWM and MPPT varies by system and conditions rather than a fixed number — but given that panels and batteries are usually the largest cost in a system, MPPT is generally the better default for anything beyond a small, simple setup.

Wire gauge is part of this too

Once you know your estimated operating current, undersized wire between panels and controller is its own fire and efficiency risk, separate from fuse sizing. Higher current at lower voltage generally requires thicker (lower AWG number) copper wire to keep voltage drop and heating within safe limits over any meaningful run length. Check your calculated current against a standard ampacity chart for your wire type and run length — don't assume the cable that came bundled with a panel is adequate for your actual installation.

Common questions

Do portable power stations need a separate MPPT controller?

Most all-in-one portable power stations (Jackery, EcoFlow, Bluetti-style units) have an MPPT controller built into the unit itself, so you generally don't need a separate external controller for panels sold as compatible accessories. This calculator is more relevant for DIY off-grid builds — vans, cabins, boats — where you're wiring your own panels into a separate battery bank and controller.

What happens if my controller is undersized?

An undersized controller will typically current-limit — capping charging below what your panels could otherwise deliver — or in some cases trip its protection circuitry or degrade faster than expected. Either way, you lose out on solar harvest you already paid for by buying the panels.

Is the array-side fuse the same as the fuse near the battery?

No — a complete system typically needs overcurrent protection on the array side (protecting the panel-to-controller wiring) and separately on the battery side (protecting the controller-to-battery wiring), since each segment carries different current and needs protection sized to that specific segment's wiring and equipment.

Why won't this calculator just give me a final fuse rating?

Because a number that ignores your panel's actual Isc, its manufacturer-specified maximum series fuse rating, and your conductor ampacity can be wrong in either direction — too small (nuisance tripping) or, more importantly, too large to actually protect the wiring in a fault condition. Getting this right means checking your specific hardware's documentation, not applying one formula to every setup.
